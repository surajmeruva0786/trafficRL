\begin{abstract}
Adaptive traffic signal control remains difficult because traffic conditions
change continuously: a nearly empty road and a saturated one require
different control decisions, yet most learning-based controllers rely on a
single policy trained to handle both. This paper presents the design and
evaluation of a Traffic-Regime Aware Multi-Head Deep Q-Network (MH-DQN), a
controller that identifies the prevailing traffic regime and applies a
policy specialized for it. The system is developed and validated across five
stages of increasing complexity and realism. Stage~1 establishes a
proof-of-concept using a standard DQN on a single four-way intersection.
Stages~2 through~5 progressively introduce the regime-aware multi-head
design, scale it to a synthetic 3 $\times$ 3 grid of nine intersections, and
finally deploy it on a real six-junction arterial corridor modeled in SUMO
from a street network in Hyderabad, India. At each stage, performance is
compared against a fixed-time baseline that alternates 30 seconds of green
for the north--south phase and 30 seconds for the east--west phase,
irrespective of demand. On the single intersection, the MH-DQN reduces
average waiting time by 73.53\% and average queue length by 41.69\% relative
to this baseline. Direct transfer of the trained model to the real-world
corridor, without additional training, reduces waiting time by 49.5\% but
incurs a 33\% reduction in throughput, a trade-off addressed through a
subsequent fine-tuning stage with an adjusted phase-change penalty. After
100 fine-tuning episodes, the corridor-deployed MH-DQN achieves a 40.2\%
reduction in average waiting time, a 28.2\% reduction in queue length, a
5.1\% increase in average speed, and only an 11.0\% reduction in throughput
under a peak demand of 2,000 vehicles per hour. These results indicate that
regime-aware policy specialization scales effectively from a single
intersection, through multi-agent grid training, to a complex real-world
multi-junction network.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

\IEEEPARstart{U}{rban} traffic congestion is a significant infrastructure
problem with economic, environmental, and social costs. Inefficient
signal timing contributes to lost productivity, unnecessary fuel
consumption, higher emissions, and reduced quality of life in metropolitan
areas~\cite{zhao2024survey,xiao2026survey}. Most intersections are still
regulated by fixed-time controllers, which allocate green time according to
a pre-set schedule regardless of actual demand. Such controllers are simple
and reliable to operate, but they cannot adapt when traffic volume rises,
falls, or shifts direction over the course of a day.

Reinforcement learning (RL) provides an alternative to this fixed-schedule
approach. Rather than following a pre-defined plan, an RL agent learns a
signal-timing policy through direct interaction with a traffic
environment, gradually improving its behavior based on observed
outcomes~\cite{mnih2015human}. By treating signal control as a sequential
decision-making problem, RL-based methods can discover control strategies
that a hand-designed schedule would not capture, particularly as traffic
conditions become more complex or unpredictable.

Most existing RL-based traffic signal controllers, however, rely on a single
policy network trained to handle all traffic conditions. This assumption
overlooks an important characteristic of urban traffic, illustrated in
Fig.~\ref{fig:traffic_regimes}: traffic exhibits qualitatively different
behavior depending on demand. A nearly empty road, a moderately congested
one, and a fully saturated one each call for a distinct control strategy. A
single policy can achieve reasonable average performance across these
conditions, but it typically underperforms a policy specialized for each
regime~\cite{yao2025moesac}.

The contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A complete, progressive development trajectory from a standard
          DQN (Stage~1) through multi-head specialization (Stage~2),
          multi-agent grid scaling (Stage~3), real-world transfer
          (Stage~4), and fine-tuning (Stage~5).
    \item The MH-DQN architecture: a shared state encoder, multiple
          regime-specialized Q-heads, and a dynamic regime classifier with
          soft gating.
    \item A quantitative analysis of the throughput-versus-waiting-time
          trade-off across all stages and the fine-tuning strategy that
          mitigates it.
    \item A demonstration of transfer learning to a six-junction
          real-world arterial corridor, achieving a 40.2\% reduction in
          waiting time under a peak demand of 2{,}000~veh/hr.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Adaptive Traffic Signal Control}

Adaptive traffic signal control (ATSC) has long been studied as an
alternative to fixed-time plans. Classical systems such as
SCOOT~\cite{hunt1982scoot} and SCATS~\cite{sims1980scats} adjust signal
splits and offsets using detector measurements and pre-computed lookup
tables. These systems improve upon static timing plans,
but they depend on hand-engineered rules that do not generalize well to
unseen demand patterns or unfamiliar network layouts. Later actuated and
self-organizing control methods introduced detector-triggered green
extensions and decentralized, pressure-based heuristics, which respond more
quickly but remain fundamentally reactive: the control law is fixed in
advance and cannot reshape itself as demand evolves over a day or across
seasons. Model-based optimization approaches, including integer programming
and model predictive control, offer theoretical guarantees but suffer from
computational intractability at scale and depend on accurate traffic models
that are difficult to maintain in practice~\cite{chen2022realdeal}. Model
predictive controllers, in particular, must re-solve a constrained
optimization problem at every decision step, and their performance degrades
sharply once the calibrated model drifts from field conditions, a recurring
issue in dense, heterogeneous urban traffic where lane discipline is weak
and turning ratios fluctuate. These limitations motivate a shift toward
data-driven, learning-based approaches that infer control policies directly
from interaction rather than from an explicit, hand-maintained model of the
network.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning for Traffic Signal Control}

Deep reinforcement learning has emerged as a prominent data-driven
alternative to the methods above. Mnih et al.~\cite{mnih2015human}
demonstrated that DQN achieves strong performance on complex sequential
decision tasks, establishing the algorithmic foundation for subsequent
RL-based traffic signal control (RL-TSC) work. Chen et
al.~\cite{chen2022realdeal} reviewed the practical barriers -- detector
noise, communication reliability, and interpretability -- that have kept
RL-TSC controllers largely out of field deployment despite strong
simulation results, while Mei et al.~\cite{mei2023libsignal} released
LibSignal, an open benchmarking library that standardizes state, reward,
and evaluation protocols across SUMO and CityFlow so published RL-TSC
methods can be compared fairly. Both works, however, evaluate each
candidate algorithm as a single policy applied uniformly across all traffic
conditions.

A related line of work improves generalization and coordination rather
than the traffic-specific formulation. Jiang et al.~\cite{jiang2024xlight}
proposed X-Light, a transformer-on-transformer meta-reinforcement-learning
architecture that transfers a shared policy across cities with
heterogeneous intersection layouts, and Yuan et
al.~\cite{yuan2025collmlight} introduced CoLLMLight, which coordinates
signal decisions network-wide using cooperating large language model agents
reasoning over a spatiotemporal traffic graph. Zhang et
al.~\cite{zhang2026equity} proposed MA2B-DDQN, an action-branching double
DQN that decomposes a corridor into per-intersection and network-wide
action branches to optimize traveler-level equity. Each of these systems
scales an RL-TSC policy along a different axis -- across cities, across
coordinating agents, or across equity objectives -- but retains a single
policy, or a single policy per branch, that must cover the full range of
traffic demand it encounters, from a nearly empty approach to a fully
saturated one.

Two recent surveys reach the same conclusion from a broader vantage point.
Zhao et al.~\cite{zhao2024survey} review the DRL-TSC literature by state
design, reward formulation, and algorithm family, and Xiao et
al.~\cite{xiao2026survey} extend this taxonomy with the most recent
architecture-level developments; both note that the large majority of
published RL-TSC agents still commit to a single value network expected to
cover the entire operating envelope. Outside traffic signal control, Yao et
al.~\cite{yao2025moesac} show that a mixture-of-experts controller --
gated between a reinforcement-learning expert and a heuristic expert --
outperforms a single soft-actor-critic policy on highway maneuver decisions
for connected and automated vehicles, evidence that specialized sub-policies
generalize better than a monolithic one when the underlying decision
context is itself heterogeneous, a property directly applicable to
multi-regime traffic. Table~\ref{tab:related} summarizes these approaches
and their limitations. The framework proposed in this paper addresses this
gap by introducing a regime-aware traffic signal control approach for
intelligent networked transportation systems.

standard DQN as a proof-of-concept on a single four-way intersection and
advancing through multi-head specialization, multi-agent grid scaling, and
ultimately real-world corridor deployment with fine-tuning. This staged
approach enables validation at each level of complexity before proceeding to
the next, so that observed performance gains can be attributed to the
specific architectural change introduced at each stage. Fig.~\ref{fig:system_overview} presents the generic system architecture and

\textbf{Episode budget rationale.}
Stages~1--3 are each allocated 200 training episodes because the agents
begin from random initialization ($\epsilon$ decays from 1.0 to 0.05) and
require sufficient exploration time to encounter the full range of queue and
demand conditions. Stages~4 and~5 are limited to 100 episodes each, since
they constitute fine-tuning rather than training from scratch: every
junction agent is initialized with pre-trained Stage~2 weights, which
already encode useful representations of queue dynamics. Extending training
beyond 100 episodes risks catastrophic forgetting of these generalizable
representations.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}

We model adaptive traffic signal control as a Markov decision process
(MDP) $(S, A, P, R, \gamma)$~\cite{suttonbarto2018}.

\textbf{State space $S$:}
\[
s = [\underbrace{q_N, q_S, q_E, q_W}_{\text{queue counts}},\;
     \underbrace{w_N, w_S, w_E, w_W}_{\text{waiting times}},\;
     \underbrace{\phi}_{\text{phase}}] \in \mathbb{R}^9
\]
where $q_i\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq0}$ is the number of queued vehicles on
approach $i\in\{N,S,E,W\}$, $w_i\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}$ is the waiting time
(in seconds) on approach $i$, and $\phi(s)\in\{0,1\}$ is the phase bit
denoting the signal phase active when $s$ was observed (0: NS green; 1: EW
green), consistent with the action encoding defined below. The four queue
counts capture spatial demand on each approach; the four waiting times
convey temporal urgency; and $\phi(s)$ lets the network condition its
decision on the phase currently in effect. This compact 9-dimensional
representation contains sufficient information to classify a traffic
regime, assess urgency, and avoid unnecessary phase changes, while
remaining low-dimensional enough for stable DQN training.

\textbf{Action space $A$:} two discrete actions per junction: $a=0$ (NS
green) and $a=1$ (EW green).

\textbf{Transition dynamics $P$:} $P(s'\mid s,a)$ is determined by
stochastic vehicle arrival processes and network routing in
SUMO~\cite{lopez2018microscopic,krajzewicz2012sumo}. It is not
known analytically; the agent learns purely from sampled $(s,a,r,s')$
transitions stored in a replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$.

\textbf{Reward function $R$:} the reward is a weighted combination of four
terms computed at each control step:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:reward}
r = -0.1\,\hat{w} - 0.1\,\hat{q} + 1.0\,c - \lambda\,\delta(s,a)
\end{equation}
where $\hat{w}\in[0,1]$ is the normalized average waiting time per vehicle
across the network; $\hat{q}\in[0,1]$ is the normalized fraction of
vehicles currently queued (halted) in the network; $c\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq0}$
is the throughput term, the number of vehicles that complete their route
during the current control step; and
$\delta(s,a) = \mathbb{1}[a\neq\phi(s)]\in\{0,1\}$ is a phase-switch
indicator, equal to 1 exactly when the selected action $a$ differs from the
phase $\phi(s)$ encoded in the state, i.e., when the agent switches the
active phase. The negative coefficients on $\hat{w}$ and $\hat{q}$ penalize
congestion, the positive coefficient on $c$ rewards throughput, and
$\lambda$ penalizes switching through $\delta(s,a)$ ($\lambda=0.05$ in
Stages~1--4; $\lambda=1.0$ in Stage~5). This reward formulation, and in
particular the phase-switch indicator $\delta(s,a)$ tied directly to the
state's phase bit $\phi(s)$, is specific to the regime-aware controller
developed in this work and is held fixed across all five stages except for
$\lambda$.

\textbf{Discount factor $\gamma$:} $\gamma=0.99$ in Stage~1 (standard DQN)
and $\gamma=0.95$ in Stages~2--5 (MH-DQN). This slight reduction in later
stages prioritizes near-term congestion relief and stabilizes training under
a multi-objective reward with regime-switching dynamics.

\subsection{Multi-Head DQN Architecture}

The MH-DQN architecture comprises three tightly coupled components.
Fig.~\ref{fig:mhdqn_arch} illustrates the complete architecture.

\textbf{1) Shared State Encoder.}
Two fully-connected layers (256 units, ReLU), shared across all Q-heads and
the regime classifier. This shared trunk (i) reduces total parameter count
relative to three fully independent networks, (ii) prevents redundant
feature learning, and (iii) makes transfer learning straightforward: the
shared encoder weights from Stage~2 are directly reused in Stages~4 and~5.

\textbf{2) Multiple Q-Heads.}
Three specialized Q-value networks (128 units, ReLU; linear output of
$|A|=2$), one per traffic regime:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low-regime head ($\theta_1$):} queue $\leq 3$ and
          wait $\leq 10$\,s. Learns to avoid unnecessary phase changes
          under sparse, free-flow arrivals.
    \item \textbf{Medium-regime head ($\theta_2$):} queue $\leq 8$ and
          wait $\leq 25$\,s. Learns balanced timing that clears moderate
          queues without starving any approach.
    \item \textbf{High-regime head ($\theta_3$):} queue $>8$ or
          wait $>25$\,s. Learns aggressive queue-clearing to prevent
          cascading delays under saturation.
\end{itemize}
These queue- and waiting-time thresholds are not adopted from prior
literature; they were obtained empirically, through iterative
experimentation (trial and error) while developing and tuning the model
on the simulation setups used in this paper, and were chosen to yield a
reasonable, well-populated split of low-, medium-, and high-demand traffic
states for these specific environments.

Each head maintains its own parameters $\theta_k$ and target network
$\theta_k^{-}$, enabling independent specialization without gradient
interference between regimes.

\textbf{3) Traffic Regime Classifier and Soft Gating.}
A lightweight classifier ($128\to64\to3$, softmax) takes the encoder output
and produces regime probabilities $p_k(s)$ for $k=1,\dots,K$, with $K=3$
regimes (Low, Medium, High, indexed in that order, i.e., $k=1,2,3$
respectively) satisfying $\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_k(s)=1$; the classifier is
trained jointly with the Q-heads. In Stages~1--2, \emph{hard switching}
($\arg\max_k p_k(s)$) selects a single head. From Stage~4 onward,
\emph{soft gating} blends all three heads through the following
aggregation rule, proposed as part of this architecture:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:blend}
Q_{\text{blend}}(s,a) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} p_k(s)\cdot Q(s,a;\,\theta_k)
\end{equation}
where $Q(s,a;\theta_k)$ is the Q-value produced by regime head $k$ defined
above and $Q_{\text{blend}}(s,a)$ is the resulting blended value from which
the greedy action $\arg\max_a Q_{\text{blend}}(s,a)$ is selected. Soft
gating is essential
for the multi-junction corridor because traffic conditions transition
continuously between regimes within a single episode. Hard switching at
regime boundaries causes abrupt policy changes that manifest as erratic
phase switching. Soft gating instead interpolates Q-values using the
classifier's confidence $p_k(s)$ as weights, producing a smooth, convex
combination of regime-specific policies. This directly accounts for
the decreasing phase-change ratio observed across stages: $2.32\times$
(Stage~2, hard switching) $\to$ $1.57\times$ (Stage~4) $\to$ $1.23\times$
(Stage~5).

\subsection{Training Objective}

The training objective is grounded in temporal difference (TD)
learning~\cite{watkins1992qlearning,mnih2015human}. Each Q-head $k$ is
trained independently, on
transitions drawn from its regime-specific subset $\mathcal{D}_k \subseteq
\mathcal{D}$ of the shared replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$, by minimizing the
mean-squared TD error:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:td}
\mathcal{L}(\theta_k) = \mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r,s')\sim\mathcal{D}_k}\!\left[\!\left(
    \underbrace{r + \gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a';\,\theta_k^{-})}_{\text{TD target (fixed)}}
    -\;\underbrace{Q(s,a;\,\theta_k)}_{\text{current prediction}}
\right)^{\!2}\right]
\end{equation}
where $Q(s,a;\theta_k)$ is the current Q-value prediction; $r$ is the
immediate reward defined in~\eqref{eq:reward}; $\gamma$ is the discount
factor; $\max_{a'}Q(s',a';\theta_k^{-})$ is the bootstrapped future value
estimated by the target network; and $\theta_k^{-}$ is a periodically
synchronized copy of $\theta_k$ (updated every 100 steps) that decouples
the regression target from the parameters being trained, preventing the
network from chasing a moving target.

All Q-heads draw from the same underlying replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$, but
each is trained only on its assigned regime subset $\mathcal{D}_k$ and
maintains independent parameter sets $\theta_k$ and target networks
$\theta_k^{-}$. Training uses Adam~\cite{kingma2015adam} (learning rate
$10^{-3}$ for
Stages~1--3; $5\times10^{-5}$ for fine-tuning in Stages~4--5) and
$\epsilon$-greedy exploration decaying from 1.0 to 0.05 (from 0.3 to 0.05
for fine-tuning stages).

The TD objective in~\eqref{eq:td} follows the standard formulation
introduced by Mnih et al.~\cite{mnih2015human}; its contribution here lies
in being applied independently to each of the $K$ regime-specialized
heads. The regime-aware reward in~\eqref{eq:reward} and the soft-gating
rule in~\eqref{eq:blend}, by contrast, are formulated specifically for the
MH-DQN architecture proposed in this paper.

\subsection{Key Observations}

The baseline DQN outperformed random exploration but revealed two
limitations that motivated the multi-head approach: (1) a single policy
struggled to adapt optimally across the full traffic demand spectrum within
an episode, and (2) reward shaping required explicit balancing between
throughput and congestion metrics. These observations directly motivated the
regime-aware architecture introduced in Stage~2.

\section{Stage 2 --- Multi-Head DQN (Single Intersection)}
\label{sec:stage2}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Simulation Environment}

Using the same four-way intersection (Fig.~\ref{fig:sim_stage12}), Stage~2
replaces the standard DQN with the full MH-DQN. The regime classifier
dynamically routes experiences to the appropriate Q-head during training and
blends heads during inference.

\subsection{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:results_sim} presents the full performance comparison, and
it is worth walking through what each row actually means rather than
reading the percentages in isolation. The two rows that matter most for a
vehicle waiting at this intersection are average waiting time and average
queue length: the MH-DQN cuts waiting time by 73.53\% (0.24\,s vs.\
0.92\,s) and queue length by 41.69\% (2.21 vs.\ 3.79). In practical terms,
vehicles under the learned policy are rarely made to wait at all, compared
to the fixed-time controller's already modest delays.

This improvement is not free. The MH-DQN switches phases far more often
than the fixed-time controller does (253.3 vs.\ 109.0 changes per episode,
roughly $2.32\times$ as many), and this shows up in two places in the
table. First, throughput falls by 12.89\% (1129.78 vs.\ 1297.00
vehicles/hr, mirrored in the Vehicles Completed row): each extra phase
change costs a brief all-red clearance period during which no vehicles can
move, so clearing queues faster by switching more often comes at the cost
of processing fewer vehicles overall. Second, and easy to miss at a
glance, the MH-DQN's average reward is actually \emph{lower} than the
fixed-time baseline's (1095.78 vs.\ 1253.46). Taken alone, this looks like
a contradiction, since the MH-DQN clearly gives the better outcome for any
individual vehicle. The explanation lies in the reward formula itself
(\eqref{eq:reward}): throughput carries a coefficient of $+1.0$, an order
of magnitude larger than the $-0.1$ coefficients on waiting time and queue
length. Because the fixed-time controller processes more vehicles per
episode, its throughput term alone outweighs the small waiting-time and
queue-length penalties it accumulates, giving it a higher raw reward even
though it delivers a worse experience to every vehicle passing through.
This is an important, honest caveat to state plainly: reward is the
training signal used to shape the agent's behavior, not the metric this
paper uses to judge success. Every headline result reported in this paper
is judged directly by waiting time, queue length, throughput, and speed,
precisely because raw reward can be misleading in exactly this way.

Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion_train} and Fig.~\ref{fig:training} deserve a
careful, honest reading, because taken at face value they appear to
contradict the 100\% evaluation accuracy reported above. The training-time
confusion matrix (Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion_train}) shows the classifier
predicting Low, Medium, and High in almost the same proportion (33\%,
42\%, 25\%) regardless of the true regime, and the classifier-accuracy
curve in Fig.~\ref{fig:training} stays close to chance level (around
0.33--0.35) for essentially the entire 200-episode run, even while the
classifier loss in the same figure collapses to nearly zero within the
first few episodes. The reason is not that the classifier fails to learn.
Both of these training-time metrics are running averages computed over
\emph{every} prediction recorded since episode~1, rather than over a
recent window. Because the early part of training is dominated by
near-random exploration ($\epsilon$ close to 1.0) and a classifier that
has barely begun to learn, a large volume of near-chance predictions
accumulates early on, and this early data continues to weigh down the
cumulative average for the rest of the run, even after the classifier has,
in fact, converged. This is confirmed directly by the evaluation-time
confusion matrix (Fig.~\ref{fig:confusion}), which is computed only after
training is complete and shows a perfect diagonal. Read side by side
rather than in isolation, these figures show that regime classification is
unreliable during the early, exploratory part of training and only
becomes fully dependable by the time training finishes, which is exactly
what the evaluation-time accuracy reflects.

The remaining panels of Fig.~\ref{fig:training} describe the underlying
training process in more familiar terms. The Q-loss rises briefly as the
network first begins to bootstrap from its own predictions, then
gradually falls and settles into a stable, low range past roughly
episode~80, a standard signature of a value function converging rather
than diverging or oscillating. Episode reward drifts gently upward over
the same period, and average waiting time, while noisy from episode to
episode, shows no worsening trend, together indicating a training run
that is stable rather than fragile.

\subsection{Regime-Specific Performance}

Consistent mean rewards (0.37--0.39) across all regimes indicate successful
Q-head specialization. For each regime, the mean per-step reward its head
achieved, the standard deviation of that reward, and the number of states
it was evaluated on are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low:}    Mean reward = 0.37, Std = 0.57 (8{,}029 states)
    \item \textbf{Medium:} Mean reward = 0.39, Std = 0.59 (12{,}515 states)
    \item \textbf{High:}   Mean reward = 0.38, Std = 0.59 (7{,}852 states)
\end{itemize}
This closeness is a genuinely encouraging result and worth pausing on. The
high-traffic regime is, by construction, the hardest of the three to
control well, since queues are longer and the risk of spillback is
greater; if the high-regime head were struggling relative to the others,
its mean reward would be visibly lower than theirs. Instead, its mean
reward (0.38) is essentially the same as the low-regime head's (0.37),
which indicates that specialization is doing its job: the high-regime head
has learned a policy well matched to the harder conditions it faces,
rather than merely making the best of an inherently worse situation. The
similarly sized standard deviations (0.57--0.59) further suggest that no
regime is disproportionately noisy or unstable relative to the others.

\subsection{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:grid} shows how training unfolds across the 200 episodes. A
first thing worth noting honestly is that reward stays negative
throughout: it starts around $-629$ at the very first episode, reaches a
best value of $-441.5$ at episode~113, and settles in the $-485$ range by
the end of training (Ep.~190--200). This is expected rather than
concerning. Nine intersections generate far more total waiting time and
queueing than a single intersection ever could, so the negative waiting
and queue terms in the reward naturally dominate the positive throughput
term, keeping overall reward negative even as the policy genuinely
improves. What matters is the direction of travel, not the sign: reward
rises substantially from the worst episode to the best, and throughput
grows 4.3\% from early to late training, both pointing to the same
conclusion, that the nine independent agents are actually learning to
manage their local intersections better over time, rather than the grid
simply becoming more congested and the reward numbers drifting for
unrelated reasons.

A second, and arguably more important, finding is that the coordination
score remains at 0.0 for the entire run. This score is intended to capture
any additional reward the agents earn specifically for demonstrably
synchronizing with one another; a persistent 0.0 means this explicit bonus
was never triggered, and every improvement in reward and throughput
reported above came from each junction's own agent learning to serve its
own approaches better, with no direct signal ever telling neighboring
agents to cooperate. That the grid still improves under these conditions
is an encouraging scalability result: it suggests that acceptable
multi-junction behavior does not require an explicit coordination
mechanism, since independent agents adapting to a shared, dynamic
environment already produce sensible collective behavior.

\subsection{Results}

Table~\ref{tab:results_hab4} summarizes what happens when the Stage~2

(3.11\,s to 1.57\,s) and queue length falls by 35.1\% (77.03 to 50.03),

junctions and a full episode, this lost time adds up to a substantial
share of the corridor's total capacity. In short, the phase-change penalty
used during Stage~2 training ($\lambda=0.05$) was too weak to matter much
on a single, forgiving intersection, but that same weak penalty becomes
expensive once applied across six junctions where every extra switch is
amplified by the corridor's scale. This is exactly the problem the
Stage~5 fine-tuning is designed to correct.

\subsection{Fine-Tuning Protocol and Reward Adjustments}

Table~\ref{tab:reward_changes} shows the two targeted reward parameter

Both changes push in the same direction: raising $\lambda$ from $0.05$ to

lengthening the minimum green time from 10\,s to 20\,s adds a hard
mechanical constraint that prevents the agent from switching before at
least 20\,s have elapsed, regardless of what the learned policy would
otherwise choose. Together, a stronger incentive and a stronger constraint
reinforce each other. All six junction agents are initialized with
Stage~2 pre-trained weights and fine-tuned for 100 episodes. The best
reward achieved during fine-tuning is $-357.57$.

\subsection{Training Dynamics}

Table~\ref{tab:hab_training} shows the fine-tuning progression in five
episode blocks, and Fig.~\ref{fig:training_hab} shows the same story as
continuous curves against the fixed-time baseline. Read together, they
describe a specific and honest sequence of events, rather than a simple,
uniform improvement.

In the first block (Ep.~1--10), reward starts at its best value of the
entire run ($-430.9$), while throughput is still low (858.6~veh/hr) and
phase changes are elevated. Fig.~\ref{fig:training_hab} shows why: over
these same early episodes, phase changes climb briefly even higher before
declining, because the six junction agents begin fine-tuning from Stage~2
weights that were trained under a ten-times-weaker phase-change penalty
($\lambda=0.05$) and initially continue switching out of that earlier
habit. Under the new, much larger penalty ($\lambda=1.0$), this is
expensive, and reward falls to its worst value of the run in the very
next block (Ep.~11--25, $-649.2$), even as throughput continues climbing
(893.5~veh/hr). From this point on, phase changes decline steadily toward,
and eventually below, the fixed-time baseline of 450, converging to
approximately 420--430 per episode, while throughput keeps rising past
the 927~veh/hr baseline by around episode~40 and settles above it
(968--979~veh/hr across the final three blocks). Reward itself only
partially recovers, settling in the $-608$ to $-632$ range for the rest of
training, never returning to its Ep.~1--10 level.

This is worth stating plainly rather than smoothing over: the fine-tuned
policy evaluated in the next subsection is one whose raw reward is worse
than it was in the very first few episodes, even though its real-world
behavior, measured in throughput and phase changes, is unambiguously
better. As in Stage~2, this is a consequence of how the reward is
constructed rather than a sign that fine-tuning failed: the heavier
phase-change penalty that ultimately brings switching under control also
makes the reward less forgiving overall, so a lower raw reward here is the
expected price of better-behaved, lower-switching traffic control, not
evidence against it.

\subsection{Evaluation Results}

The fine-tuned MH-DQN achieves a 40.2\% waiting time reduction
(1.9\,s vs.\ 3.1\,s) and a 28.2\% queue length reduction, alongside a 5.1\%
increase in average speed (7.3 to 7.7\,m/s): vehicles are not only waiting
less, they are moving faster while travelling through the corridor.
Critically, throughput is reduced by only 11.0\%, a three-fold improvement
over Stage~4's 33.0\% loss, and this recovery is achieved purely through
reward re-shaping (a higher phase-change penalty and a longer minimum
green time), without any modification to the network architecture itself.

One honest detail is worth flagging rather than glossing over: even after
fine-tuning, the agents still switch phases somewhat more often than the
fixed-time baseline (553.2 vs.\ 450.0, a 22.9\% increase). The stronger
penalty brings this down substantially from Stage~4's 705.2 switches, but
it does not push switching below the fixed-time baseline, and there is a
reasonable explanation for why it should not: the fixed-time controller
switches on a rigid, demand-blind 30\,s/30\,s schedule, while the MH-DQN
must still react to real, uneven traffic arriving at six different
junctions under peak demand. Some of that extra switching is simply the

\subsection{Discussion}

\textbf{Throughput trade-off and reward sensitivity.}
The throughput reduction is notably consistent between Stage~2 ($-12.89\%$)
and Stage~5 ($-11.0\%$), suggesting that this residual gap is intrinsic to
the waiting-time-focused reward rather than a byproduct of network scale.
Raising the phase penalty from $\lambda=0.05$ to $\lambda=1.0$ closes
Stage~4's $-33\%$ throughput gap to $-11\%$, recovering 30.2\% of throughput
at the cost of only $+0.29$\,s in absolute waiting time. The near-identical
residual gap observed at two very different network scales indicates that
the remaining throughput penalty is not an artifact of corridor-specific
geometry but rather a structural consequence of optimizing primarily for
delay. When the agent is rewarded chiefly for emptying queues quickly, it
favors shorter, more frequent service of whichever approach is momentarily
most congested, and this behavior trades a small amount of sustained
directional flow for lower instantaneous delay. That the penalty term alone
shifts the operating point so substantially, without any change to the
network or the encoder, underscores that the trade-off is governed by
reward geometry rather than representational capacity.

\textbf{Absolute waiting-time benefit scales with baseline congestion.}
The corridor baseline exhibits substantially higher waiting times
(3.1\,s vs.\ 0.92\,s) due to higher demand and multi-junction spillback.
The MH-DQN yields a greater absolute benefit under this more congested,
real-world scenario, saving 1.2\,s in the corridor compared with 0.68\,s at
the single intersection. This scaling behavior is practically significant
because heavily loaded, spillback-prone corridors impose the largest
aggregate delay on a road network and therefore offer the greatest
opportunity for system-level improvement. A controller whose advantage
grows with congestion is well matched to where the operational need is
most acute, in contrast to methods whose benefit is concentrated in
lightly loaded regimes that already perform acceptably under fixed
timing.

\textbf{Soft gating improves phase stability.}
The phase-change ratio drops from $2.32\times$ (Stage~2, hard switching) to
$1.57\times$ (Stage~4) to $1.23\times$ (Stage~5), an important property for
real-world systems where excessive switching introduces mechanical wear and
driver uncertainty. Beyond hardware longevity, frequent phase changes
generate repeated inter-green clearance intervals during which the
intersection serves no movement at all, so each avoided switch reclaims
capacity that would otherwise be lost to transition overhead. The monotone
decrease of the ratio as soft gating and the stronger penalty are
introduced suggests that smooth interpolation between regime-specific
policies, rather than abrupt switching at classifier boundaries, is the
mechanism most responsible for stabilizing the realized control
sequence.

\textbf{Decentralized multi-agent control is effective.}
Despite the absence of explicit inter-agent communication across
Stages~3--5, the decentralized MH-DQN achieves consistent improvements
through implicit coordination via shared simulator dynamics and upstream
demand smoothing. This is a desirable property for field deployment, where
a fully centralized controller would require reliable low-latency
communication among all junctions and would constitute a single point of
failure for the entire corridor. The decentralized formulation degrades
gracefully: an individual agent continues to operate on its own local
state even if neighboring controllers are unavailable, while still
benefiting from the demand shaping that each upstream junction imposes on
the arrivals seen downstream.

\textbf{Transfer learning enables rapid corridor adaptation.}
Even Stage~4's direct transfer achieves $-49.5\%$ waiting time and $-35.1\%$
queue length on the real-world corridor, validating the generalizability of
the regime-aware representation. That a policy trained on a single
synthetic intersection transfers usefully to a six-junction real-world
corridor before any corridor-specific fine-tuning indicates that the shared
encoder captures demand features that are largely invariant across network
scales. The subsequent fine-tuning stage then acts as a lightweight
specialization step rather than a full retraining, which is the regime in
which transfer learning is most valuable: the bulk of the representational
work is amortized once and reused, and adaptation to a new site is reduced
to a short, low-learning-rate refinement.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

Several directions emerge from this progressive development:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Reward Function Tuning:} Pareto-optimal reward
          formulations jointly optimizing waiting time and throughput could
          reduce the residual $\approx\!11\%$ throughput gap.
    \item \textit{Explicit Multi-Agent Coordination:} Graph neural networks
          or attention mechanisms for inter-agent
          communication~\cite{wei2019colight} could enable corridor-level
          coordination while maintaining waiting-time gains.
    \item \textit{Hierarchical Policy Learning:} A corridor-level policy
          coordinating junction-level agents could yield system-level
          benefits beyond individual junction optimization.
    \item \textit{Dynamic Regime Threshold Adaptation:} Adaptive regime
          boundary definitions could improve robustness across diverse
          urban environments and time-of-day demand profiles.
    \item \textit{Real-World Deployment:} Transferring SUMO-trained
          policies to real intersections with live sensor data, noise,
          communication delays, and safety constraints is the critical
          next step.
    \item \textit{Extended Corridor Networks:} Applying the framework
          city-wide through hierarchical or federated multi-agent
          extensions, building on Stage~3 scalability.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ---------------------------------------------------------------

This paper presented the complete progressive development and evaluation of
a Traffic-Regime Aware Multi-Head Deep Q-Network for adaptive traffic
signal control, tracing a five-stage trajectory from a baseline
proof-of-concept to real-world corridor deployment.

Beginning with a standard DQN on a single four-way intersection (Stage~1),
the architecture was augmented with regime-specific Q-heads and a dynamic
classifier (Stage~2), achieving a 73.53\% waiting-time reduction and a
41.69\% queue-length reduction relative to a 30\,s/30\,s fixed-time
baseline. Multi-agent scalability was validated on a $3\times3$
nine-junction grid (Stage~3). Direct transfer to the real-world
six-junction arterial corridor (Stage~4) achieved a 49.5\% waiting-time
improvement but exposed a 33\% throughput penalty. Fine-tuning with an
increased phase-change penalty and minimum green time (Stage~5) recovered
30.2\% of throughput, yielding a 40.2\% waiting-time reduction, a 28.2\%
queue reduction, a 5.1\% speed improvement, and only an 11.0\% throughput
reduction under a 2{,}000~veh/hr peak demand.

These results demonstrate that regime-aware policy specialization scales
effectively from single-intersection benchmarks, through multi-agent grid
training, to complex real-world multi-junction networks, and that
iterative reward fine-tuning is essential for balancing congestion
reduction against throughput in realistic deployment scenarios.
